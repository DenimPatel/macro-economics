## Overview

- Aggregate forces produce paradoxes that individual-level reasoning gets backwards

This lecture introduces the field of macroeconomics, distinguishing it from microeconomics, and provides a roadmap for the course. We examine the current economic environment (circa 2023): low unemployment, high inflation, rising interest rates, and volatile asset markets. The lecture demonstrates how macroeconomic forces create paradoxes like "good news is bad news" for financial markets.

## 1. What is Macroeconomics?

```summary
- **Micro** studies individual agents; **macro** studies aggregates — countries, regions, the world
- Four key variables: inflation, unemployment, exchange rates and aggregate output (GDP)
- Macro is not the sum of micros: the fallacy of composition makes individual reasoning unreliable at scale
- Individual actions interact to produce outcomes no single agent intended
- Macro works by simplification — the models are simple so the insight survives them
- Aim: understand what traders and policymakers say, including when they get it wrong
```

### Macro vs. Micro: A Fundamental Distinction
- **Microeconomics**: Studies individual agents (households, firms, industries).
    - Example: Why does a particular worker choose to work at a specific firm?
    - Example: How does Apple set prices for iPhones?
- **Macroeconomics**: Studies the economy as a whole (countries, regions, global).
    - Example: Why is the US unemployment rate at 3.5%?
    - Example: Why is inflation at 8% instead of 2%?

**Key Variables in Macroeconomics**:
- **Inflation**: Rate of change of the general price level (not individual prices).
- **Unemployment Rate**: Share of labor force without jobs (not individual employment status).
- **Exchange Rates**: Relative price of currencies (not individual goods across countries).
- **Aggregate Output (GDP)**: Total production in the economy.

### Why Macro ≠ Sum of Micros
- **Fallacy of Composition**: What is true for an individual may not be true for all individuals together.
- **Interaction Effects**: In macroeconomics, individual behaviors interact to create aggregate outcomes that cannot be understood by studying individuals in isolation.
- **Example (Preview)**: If one person saves more, they become wealthier. But if everyone tries to save more simultaneously, aggregate demand falls, causing a recession. Total savings may not increase (Paradox of Savings—covered in Lecture 3).

**Analogy from Physics**: Studying the motion of planets (macro) requires different tools than studying subatomic particles (micro). The aggregate behavior emerges from, but is not simply the sum of, individual parts.

### The Art of Macroeconomics
- **Shortcuts and Simplifications**: Macro requires simplifying complex systems to capture their essence.
- **Models over Math**: This course emphasizes intuition and applied understanding, not advanced mathematics.
    - Goal: You should be able to read and critically analyze the **IMF World Economic Outlook**, **Wall Street Journal**, **Financial Times**, and **The Economist**.
    - Goal: Understand what traders and policymakers discuss, even if they sometimes get it wrong.

**Course Philosophy**: "If it gets very complicated, I'm failing."

## 2. Current Economic Context (2023)

```summary
- **Labor market**: unemployment ~3.5% against a 5-6% norm and a 10% Great Recession peak — "overheating"
- **Wages**: rising in every sector, sharpest in hospitality, where workers did not return after COVID
- **Inflation**: 6.5-8% against a 2% target, and it moves with wages (correlation ~0.8+)
- **The wage-price spiral**: tight labor market → higher wages → higher costs → higher prices → higher wage demands
- **Monetary policy**: the Fed holds rates as its main tool, and moved 0% → 4.5%+ in under a year
- **Asset prices**: the S&P 500 rose 114% in 2021 and fell 20%+ in 2022, tracking the rate path rather than the economy
- **Wealth channel**: rate moves change asset prices, which change household spending, which changes demand — and that is the squeeze: hike to fight inflation, or risk recession
```

### A. The Labor Market: Historically Tight

#### Unemployment at Record Lows
- **Current US Unemployment Rate**: ~3.5% (historically low).
- **Historical Context**:
    - **Great Recession (2007-2009)**: Unemployment peaked at 10%.
    - **COVID-19 Recession (2020)**: Unemployment spiked to 14.7% (unprecedented peacetime level).
    - **Normal Times**: Unemployment typically around 5-6%.
    - **Current**: 3.5% is far below normal, indicating an "overheating" labor market.

**Key Insight**: Low unemployment sounds great (and it is for workers), but in macro, it signals potential problems—namely, inflation pressures.

#### Wage Growth: Surging Across Sectors
- **Aggregate Wage Growth**: Rising steadily and rapidly across all sectors.
- **Service Sectors Hit Hardest**: Accommodation and food services experiencing especially sharp wage increases.
    - **Reason**: "Labor supply" problems—many workers left these sectors during COVID (early retirement, career changes, childcare responsibilities) and have not returned.
    - **Result**: Severe labor shortages driving up wages to attract workers.

**Data**: Wage growth in service sectors exceeding 6-8% annually (well above the 2-3% typical in normal times).

### B. Inflation: Far Above Target

#### The 2% Target
- **Normal Inflation**: Central banks (Federal Reserve, European Central Bank) target **~2% inflation**.
    - Why 2%? Provides a buffer against deflation, allows for relative price adjustments, consistent with stable growth (explained later in course).
- **Current Inflation**: 6.5% - 8% depending on the measure (CPI, Core CPI, GDP Deflator—defined in Lecture 2).

#### Wage-Inflation Correlation
- **High Correlation**: Wage growth and inflation move together (correlation ~0.8+).
- **Mechanism**:
    1. Tight labor market → Firms compete for scarce workers → Wages rise.
    2. Higher wages → Higher production costs → Firms raise prices.
    3. Higher prices → Workers demand higher wages → **Wage-price spiral**.

**Key Insight**: This is why low unemployment + high wage growth is a macro problem, even though it sounds like good news for individuals.

### C. Monetary Policy: Fighting Inflation with Interest Rates

#### The Central Bank's Main Tool: Interest Rates
- **Expansionary Policy** ($i \downarrow$): Lower rates → Cheaper borrowing → More investment and consumption → Higher aggregate demand → Higher output.
- **Contractionary Policy** ($i \uparrow$): Higher rates → Expensive borrowing → Less investment and consumption → Lower aggregate demand → Lower output (and lower inflation).

#### The Fed's Recent Path
1. **Pre-COVID (2015-2019)**: Interest rates around 1.5-2.5%.
2. **COVID Shock (March 2020)**: Fed cut rates to near zero (0-0.25%) to support the economy.
    - **Zero Lower Bound**: Cannot cut rates much below 0% (explained in Lecture 6, and why it
      binds in Lecture 4's liquidity trap).
3. **2021-2022**: Inflation surged, but Fed initially slow to respond ("behind the curve").
4. **2022-2023**: Fed hiking rapidly (fastest pace since the 1980s) to combat inflation.
    - Rates increased from 0% to 4.5%+ in less than a year.

**Key Insight**: The Fed faces a trade-off—hiking rates fights inflation but also slows the economy, risking recession.

### D. Asset Prices: The Wealth Channel

#### Equity Markets and Monetary Policy
- **Index**: S&P 500 (main US equity index, tracking 500 largest companies).

**Timeline**:
1. **COVID Crash (March 2020)**: S&P 500 fell 30% as pandemic hit.
    - **Why?**: Forward-looking investors anticipated economic devastation.
2. **COVID Boom (2020-2021)**: S&P 500 surged 114% (end-2020 to end-2021), exceeding pre-pandemic levels even as the economy was still recovering.
    - **Main Driver**: Ultra-low interest rates (not just "Amazon and Tesla stories").
    - **Mechanism**: Low $i$ → Less discounting of future profits → Higher stock valuations (explained in Lecture 22-23).
3. **2022 Decline**: S&P 500 fell 20%+ as Fed hiked rates.
    - **Main Driver**: Rising interest rates (can explain 100% of the decline).

**Wealth Channel of Monetary Policy**:
- Low rates → Asset prices rise → Households feel richer → Consumption increases → Aggregate demand rises.
- High rates → Asset prices fall → Households feel poorer → Consumption decreases → Aggregate demand falls.

**Key Insight**: Monetary policy affects the real economy significantly through asset prices, not just through borrowing costs.

## 3. "Good News is Bad News": The Friday Jobs Report Paradox

```summary
- January 2023: payrolls beat by a wide margin (500,000+ against 190,000 expected) and S&P futures fell 2% in minutes
- **The mechanism**: strong jobs → labor market still hot → wages still rising → inflation stays up → the Fed keeps hiking → valuations fall
- Markets are forward-looking: they price the *policy response*, not the data
- Before the report, cuts were priced for mid-2023; after it, for end-2023 or later
- In an overheating economy, strong data is bad for asset prices — that is the point of this lecture
```

### The Event (January 2023)
- **Expected**: Nonfarm payroll to increase by 190,000 jobs.
- **Actual**: Nonfarm payroll increased by **500,000+ jobs** (massive beat).
- **Stock Market Response**: S&P 500 futures crashed 2% in minutes after the 8:30 AM release.

### The Paradox
- **Real Economy News**: Wonderful—lots of jobs created, low unemployment.
- **Financial Market Reaction**: Catastrophic—equity prices plummeted.

**Why?**

#### Mechanism
1. **Strong Jobs Report** → Labor market still overheating.
2. **Overheating Labor Market** → Wage pressures remain high (recall wage-inflation correlation).
3. **High Wage Growth** → Inflation will stay elevated.
4. **Persistent Inflation** → **Fed must keep hiking rates** (or hike even more than previously expected).
5. **Higher Interest Rates** → Lower asset valuations (discounting effect) → **Stocks fall**.

#### Forward-Looking Markets
- **Financial Markets** anticipate future policy changes.
- Before the jobs report, markets expected the Fed to **start cutting rates in 4 months** (mid-2023).
- After the jobs report, markets pushed out rate cuts to **end of 2023** (or later).
- **Result**: "Good news" about jobs is "bad news" for financial assets because it implies tighter monetary policy ahead.

**Key Insight**: In an overheating economy, strong economic data is bad for asset prices because it signals the Fed will maintain restrictive policy longer.

## 4. Global Context: A Worldwide Phenomenon

```summary
- Pre-COVID, 70-80% of countries had inflation at or below 2%; by 2023 almost none did — even Japan
- **US**: demand-driven, from fiscal stimulus, monetary easing and pent-up savings
- **Europe**: the same demand pressure plus supply shocks, with energy the sharpest — war cut Russian gas
- **Latin America**: commodity exporters gained on prices while still importing inflation
- Common cause: every region over-heated out of COVID on massive fiscal and monetary support
```

### Inflation is Everywhere
- **Pre-COVID (2010-2019)**: 70-80% of countries had inflation at or below 2%.
- **Today (2023)**: Nearly **zero countries** have inflation below 2%.
    - Even **Japan**, which struggled with deflation for decades, now has inflation above 2%.

### Regional Variations
1. **United States**: Inflation driven by **aggregate demand** (fiscal stimulus, monetary easing, pent-up savings).
2. **Europe**: Similar demand-driven inflation, plus **supply shocks** from Ukraine war.
    - **Energy Prices**: War disrupted natural gas supplies (Russia was major supplier) → Energy prices surged → Inflation spiked.
3. **Latin America**: Commodity exporters benefiting from high prices but also experiencing imported inflation.

**Common Thread**: All regions "overheated" coming out of COVID due to massive fiscal and monetary stimulus.

## 5. China: The Major Exception

```summary
- China's growth fell from 5.5-6% pre-COVID to ~3% in 2022 as Zero-COVID closed factories and collapsed demand
- The reopening released forced savings and a pent-up desire to spend on services
- A 1% rise in Chinese growth adds +0.3-0.4% in East Asia and +0.4-0.5% in Latin America — the largest effect
- Latin America is hit hardest because it exports the copper, soybeans and oil China consumes
- **Double-edged**: good for global growth, but it adds demand just as central banks are trying to remove it
```

### Zero-COVID Policy and Economic Slowdown
- **Typical Growth Rate**: China grew at 5.5-6% annually (pre-COVID); much higher (~10%+) 15 years ago.
- **2022 Growth**: ~3% (or less)—significant slowdown.
- **Cause**: Aggressive **Zero-COVID policy** shut down large parts of the economy.
    - Factories closed, supply chains disrupted, domestic demand collapsed.

### The Reopening (Late 2022/Early 2023)
- **Policy Shift**: China abandoned Zero-COVID policy.
- **Expected Boom**: Massive pent-up demand (same dynamic as US post-COVID).
    - Consumers have **forced savings** (couldn't spend during lockdowns).
    - Desire to spend on services (restaurants, travel, entertainment).

### Global Spillovers: China's Growth Matters

**Impact of 1% Increase in China's Growth Rate** (by region):
- **East Asia**: +0.3-0.4% growth (neighbors benefit from trade).
- **Latin America**: +0.4-0.5% growth (**largest impact**).
    - **Why?**: Latin America exports commodities (copper, soybeans, oil); China consumes these when building/expanding.

### The Double-Edged Sword
- **Good News**: China's reopening boosts global growth, particularly for commodity exporters.
- **Bad News**: China's boom adds to global **aggregate demand** at a time when other countries are trying to **reduce demand** to fight inflation.
    - **Result**: China's reopening could fuel **more global inflation**, complicating the Fed's (and other central banks') job.

**Key Insight**: In an interconnected world, "what happens in China doesn't stay in China." Spillovers are large and asymmetric.

## 6. Recession Risk: The Cost of Disinflation

```summary
- Professional forecasters put the 2023 recession probability at a median 65%
- High rates slow investment, consumption and hiring — at some point the Fed breaks something
- The trade-off is two-sided: too little hiking leaves inflation, too much causes severe recession
- Precedent: the 1980s and early-2000s hiking cycles were each followed by recession
```

### Forecasts for 2023
- **Survey of Professional Forecasters**: Median expectation is **65% probability of recession** in 2023.
- **Reason**: The Fed (and other central banks) must hike rates aggressively to fight inflation.
    - High rates slow investment, consumption, and hiring.
    - At some point, the Fed may "break something"—cause a recession.

**Key Trade-Off**:
- Don't hike enough → Inflation persists (or worsens).
- Hike too much → Severe recession.

**Historical Precedent**: Every time the Fed has hiked rates rapidly to fight inflation (1980s, early 2000s), a recession followed shortly after.

## 7. Course Structure and Goals

```summary
- **Short run**: output set by aggregate demand — goods market, financial markets, IS-LM
- **Medium run**: prices adjust, unemployment dynamics, the inflation-unemployment trade-off
- **Long run**: growth theory — what sets living standards over decades
- **Open economy**: trade, exchange rates, spillovers (Mundell-Fleming); **financial markets**: asset pricing and expectations
- Each lecture opens on current events, builds a model, then applies it to a live policy debate
- Explicitly *not* a PhD pipeline: that needs far more mathematics
```

### What You Will Learn
1. **Short Run**: Output determined by aggregate demand (goods market, financial markets, IS-LM model).
2. **Medium Run**: Prices adjust, unemployment dynamics, inflation-unemployment trade-offs.
3. **Long Run**: Growth theory—what determines living standards over decades (capital, technology).
4. **Open Economy**: Trade, exchange rates, international spillovers (Mundell-Fleming model).
5. **Financial Markets**: Asset pricing, role of expectations, monetary policy transmission.

### Lecture Format
- **First 5-10 minutes**: Discuss current events (once you have the tools to understand them).
    - Repetition: Same topics revisited as your knowledge deepens.
- **Main Lecture**: Build models to explain the phenomena.
- **Applications**: Connect models to real-world policy debates.

### Course Philosophy
- **Simple Models**: Capture the essence of complex phenomena without complicated math.
- **Critical Thinking**: Understand what's in the news, spot mistakes in analysis.
- **Practical Skills**: Useful for internships (Wall Street, policy work), reading economic reports (IMF, WSJ).

**Not a Goal**: Make you a PhD researcher in macro (that requires much more math).
