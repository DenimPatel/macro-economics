# Lecture 17: Open Economy Macroeconomics: Introduction

## Overview

```summary
- Moves from closed-economy analysis to an open one, where goods, capital and prices all cross borders
- Three dimensions of openness: goods markets, financial markets and factor markets
- **Nominal exchange rate $E$** prices the currency; **real exchange rate $\epsilon$** prices the goods
- Business cycles move together across countries, so shocks and policies in a large economy reach everyone
- Framed by the US economy after rapid rate hikes and banking sector stress
```
This lecture transitions from closed economy analysis to open economy macroeconomics. It introduces the key concepts of openness in goods and financial markets, defines exchange rates (nominal and real), and explains why international linkages matter for macroeconomic policy and business cycles. The lecture also provides context on the current state of the US economy following rapid interest rate hikes and banking sector stress.

## 1. Current Economic Context (2023): Banking Crisis and Credit Crunch

```summary
- Post-COVID demand recovered faster than supply, opening a positive output gap ($Y > Y^*$) and driving inflation
- The Fed called inflation transitory in late 2021, delayed tightening, then hiked unusually fast in 2022-2023
- SVB's mechanism: depositors fled to money market funds and Treasuries, long bonds lost value, and lending was cut to cover withdrawals
- Small and regional banks bite disproportionately, because small businesses have no bond-market alternative
- **Credit crunch as a risk premium**: in the IS-LM-PC model it appears as a rise in $x$, and it does part of the Fed's tightening for it
- Supply-side relief: participation, the employment-to-population ratio and immigration are recovering, and wage growth is down from ~6% toward a sustainable ~3.5%
- The Fed slowed its hikes from 50bp expected to 25bp actual in March 2023
```

### Post-COVID Economic Dynamics
- **Demand-Supply Imbalance**: Following COVID reopening, demand recovered faster than supply.
    - **Strong Demand**: Accumulated savings, fiscal stimulus, pent-up consumption (people "fed up of staying at home").
    - **Weak Supply**: Supply chain bottlenecks, labor force participation below pre-COVID levels, sectoral labor shortages.
    - **Result**: Positive output gap ($Y > Y^*$) $\rightarrow$ Inflationary pressure.

### Fed's Policy Response
- **Initial Stance (Late 2021)**: Fed viewed inflation as "transitory."
    - Expected supply to recover quickly and demand to normalize naturally.
    - Delayed raising interest rates despite rising inflation.
- **Policy Error**: Inflation proved persistent (new shocks like Ukraine war + sustained demand).
- **Rapid Tightening (2022-2023)**: Fed hiked rates unusually aggressively.
    - **Gradualism Abandoned**: Typically, central banks raise rates slowly to avoid "breaking something."
    - **Risk**: Fast rate hikes strain the financial system, especially banks.

### Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) Collapse (March 2023)
- **What Broke**: Regional and small banks experienced severe deposit outflows (bank runs).
- **Mechanism**:
    1. **Deposit Outflows**: As rates rose, depositors moved funds to higher-yielding money market funds and US Treasuries.
    2. **Initially Gradual**: Large banks handled this well (diversified funding sources).
    3. **Acceleration (2023)**: Small/medium banks saw sharp, sudden deposit declines.
    4. **Asset-Side Losses**: Banks held long-term bonds (purchased when rates were low); rising rates caused market value losses.
    5. **Credit Crunch**: To cover deposit withdrawals, banks had to sell assets at losses and cut lending sharply.

### Impact on the Economy
- **Flight to Quality**: Deposits moved from small banks to large banks (and out of banking system entirely).
- **Differential Impact**: Small/regional banks are critical lenders to **small businesses** (no access to bond markets).
    - Large corporations have alternatives: bond issuance, commercial paper, multiple bank relationships.
    - Small businesses rely exclusively on bank loans (or family financing).
- **Sectoral Exposure**: Construction and services sectors have high concentrations of small businesses.
    - These sectors face disproportionate credit tightening.
- **Modeling Implication**: In IS-LM-PC framework, credit crunch appears as an **increase in the risk premium $x$**.

### Policy Implications
- **Automatic Tightening**: Credit crunch does part of Fed's work (reducing aggregate demand).
- **Fed Response**: Reduced pace of rate hikes (from 50bp expected to 25bp actual in March 2023).
- **Outlook**: Economy expected to slow significantly (possibly recession avoided if financial stress contained).

### Labor Market Silver Lining
- **Participation Rate Recovery**: Labor force participation gradually returning to pre-COVID trend.
    - Previously suppressed recovery was a key Fed forecasting error.
- **Employment-to-Population Ratio**: Approaching pre-COVID trend.
- **Immigration Rebound**: Immigration flows (critical for US labor supply) have normalized after COVID disruptions.
    - Particularly important for accommodation, food services, and other sectors with high immigrant workforce shares.
- **Wage Pressure Declining**: Wage inflation peaked at ~6% (incompatible with 2% inflation target) but now moderating.
    - Sustainable wage growth: ~3.5% (2% inflation + 1.5% productivity growth).
    - Current level (~4-4.5%) still too high but improving.

**Key Insight**: Even with low unemployment, expanding labor supply (via participation and immigration) can reduce inflationary pressure without requiring recession.

## 2. Why Open Economy Macroeconomics Matters

```summary
- The US is the most closed major economy, so most of its dynamics can be read without reference to the dollar
- For almost every other country trade, capital flows and the exchange rate are primary drivers — Singapore cannot be analysed without them
- Japan's size does not exempt it: the yen sits at the centre of the analysis
- US openness has risen steadily in trade and more steeply still in cross-border financial holdings
- Exposure to the rest of the world is the rule rather than the exception, and the US's own exposure has grown over time — though deglobalization now pushes back
```

### The US as a "Closed Economy"
- **Unique Position**: US is the **most closed** major economy in the world.
    - Can describe most US economic dynamics without referencing the rest of the world.
    - Exchange rate (dollar) plays a secondary role compared to other countries.
- **Recent Lecture**: Discussed banking crisis, inflation, Fed policy without mentioning exchange rates or foreign economies.

### Open Economy Imperative for Other Countries
- **Singapore Example**: Cannot analyze Singapore's economy without considering global trade and capital flows.
- **Even Japan**: Despite being a large economy, the yen's value is central to economic analysis.
- **General Rule**: For almost all countries, external factors (trade, capital flows, exchange rates) are **primary** drivers.

### Growing US Openness
- **Historical Trend**: US has become more integrated with global economy over time.
    - Trade (exports + imports) as share of GDP has risen steadily (until recent "deglobalization" pushback).
    - Financial market integration even larger (cross-border asset holdings).
- **Future Outlook**: Uncertain due to post-COVID deglobalization trends, but openness still significant.

## 3. Three Dimensions of Openness

```summary
- **Openness ratio** is $(X + IM)/GDP$: about 15% each way for the US, near 30% of GDP, and it understates true openness because even domestically made goods face foreign competition
- **Trade balance**: $NX = X - IM$, and the US runs a chronic deficit of about USD 70 billion a month in 2023 — a savings-investment imbalance, not a competitiveness failure
- The largest deficits run to China (around USD 380 billion a year) and Mexico (USD 130 billion); Vietnam's is largely relocated Chinese supply chains, and Germany runs a surplus
- **Financial openness dwarfs trade**: foreigners hold more than USD 20 trillion of US assets, about USD 2 trillion each from Japan, China and the UK — private money in Tokyo, London and Ottawa, central bank reserves in Beijing
- **The exorbitant privilege**: foreigners buy safe Treasuries and the US buys risky foreign assets — Brazilian sovereign bonds yield 10-14% against 3-4% on Treasuries — so a trade deficit can be funded by an investment income surplus
- **Factor mobility** — immigration and FDI — is real but out of scope here; goods and financial markets are the focus
- Tariffs and quotas throttle trade; capital controls throttle finance — rare in developed economies, routine in emerging markets
```

### 1. Openness in Goods Markets
**Definition**: Ability to import goods/services from abroad and export domestically-produced goods/services.

#### Measurement
- **Openness Ratio**: $\frac{\text{Exports} + \text{Imports}}{\text{GDP}}$
    - US: ~15% of GDP in imports, ~15% in exports (total ~30%).
    - **Underestimates True Openness**: Many non-traded goods (e.g., domestically produced cars) face international competition (prices determined by foreign competitors).
        - Example: Ford prices reflect Toyota competition, even if Ford is produced domestically.
        - Sectors with no international competition: haircuts, local services.

#### Trade Balance
- **Definition**: $NX = X - IM$ (Net Exports = Exports - Imports).
- **US Pattern**: Chronic trade deficit (~$70 billion/month in 2023).
    - Exports less than imports persistently.
- **Political Concern**: Trade deficits often criticized as "unfair competition."
    - **Economic Reality**: Reflects **savings-investment imbalance**, not competitiveness per se.
        - US saves less than it invests $\Rightarrow$ Must borrow from abroad $\Rightarrow$ Trade deficit.

#### Top US Trade Partners (Deficits)
1. **China**: Largest deficit (~$380 billion/year).
    - Major political tensions during "global imbalances" era (2000s-2010s).
2. **Mexico**: ~$130 billion/year deficit.
3. **Vietnam**: Often "Chinese exports in disguise" (supply chain relocation).
4. **Germany**: Persistent surplus country.

#### Impediments to Goods Market Openness
- **Tariffs**: Taxes on imports (reduce trade flows).
- **Quotas**: Quantitative restrictions on import volumes.

### 2. Openness in Financial Markets
**Definition**: Ability to buy/sell foreign financial assets (stocks, bonds, etc.).

#### Scale
- **Far Larger than Goods Trade**: Financial transactions dwarf goods trade by an order of magnitude.
- **US Asset Holdings by Foreigners**: ~$20+ trillion.
    - Top holders: Japan, China, UK, Canada (~$2 trillion each).
    - **Composition**:
        - Japan, UK, Canada: Mostly private sector (pension funds, wealth managers).
        - China: Primarily central bank (foreign reserves, currency intervention).

#### US Holdings of Foreign Assets
- **Substantial but Asymmetric**: US residents hold large amounts of Canadian, European, Asian, Latin American assets.

#### The "Exorbitant Privilege"
- **Asymmetry in Asset Composition**:
    - **Foreigners Buy from US**: Safe assets (US Treasuries) for insurance/safety (low yield).
    - **US Buys Abroad**: Risky assets (equity, FDI, emerging market bonds) for high returns.
- **Result**: US earns **higher average return** on foreign assets than it pays on foreign holdings of US assets.
    - **Implication**: US can run persistent trade deficits funded by investment income surplus.
    - Example: Brazilian sovereign bonds yield 10-14% vs. US Treasuries at 3-4%.

#### Impediments to Financial Openness
- **Capital Controls**: Government restrictions on cross-border capital flows.
    - **Rare in Developed Economies**: US, Europe, Japan have fully open capital accounts.
    - **Common in Emerging Markets**: Used to prevent destabilizing capital flight or sudden inflows.
        - Example: Limit outflows during crises; limit inflows during booms to prevent reversal.

### 3. Openness in Factor Markets
**Definition**: Mobility of labor and capital across borders.

#### Labor Mobility
- **Immigration**: Workers move from one country to another.
    - Critical for US labor market (as discussed earlier: 500,000/year shortfall during COVID).

#### Capital Mobility
- **Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)**: Firms relocate production facilities.
    - Example: Japanese car manufacturers (Toyota, Honda) build plants in US/Canada to serve North American market (avoid trade costs).

#### Scope of This Course
- **Focus**: Goods and financial market openness.
- **Factor Mobility**: Not covered in detail (though implicit in some discussions).

## 4. Exchange Rates: Nominal vs. Real

```summary
- **Nominal exchange rate $E$** is the price of domestic currency in foreign currency — here yen or pounds per dollar
- **Appreciation** ($E \uparrow$) makes domestic currency more valuable, **depreciation** ($E \downarrow$) makes it cheaper — the dollar ran from ~100 to ~150 yen per dollar in 2022-2023, a 50% rise on Fed tightening against a near-zero Bank of Japan
- $E$ alone cannot settle where to buy; you also need prices on both sides of the border
- **Real exchange rate** $\epsilon = E P / P^*$ is how many foreign goods one unit of domestic goods buys, and it — not $E$ — is what determines trade flows
- Worked example: a $10,000 US car against a £8,000 UK car at 0.80 £/$ gives $\epsilon = 1$, so one US car buys exactly one UK car; a dearer UK car puts $\epsilon$ below 1 and a cheaper one above
- **Real appreciation** ($\epsilon \uparrow$) follows nominal appreciation or faster domestic inflation: exports fall and imports rise; **real depreciation** reverses both
- Almost all short-run movement in $\epsilon$ comes from $E$, because $P$ and $P^*$ adjust slowly
```

### Nominal Exchange Rate ($E$)

#### Definition
- **Convention Used in This Course**: Price of **domestic currency** in terms of **foreign currency**.
    - If US is domestic: $E = $ Yen per Dollar (or Pounds per Dollar, etc.).
    - Example: $E = 130$ ¥/$ means 1 dollar buys 130 yen.

#### Appreciation vs. Depreciation
- **Appreciation** ($E \uparrow$): Domestic currency becomes more valuable.
    - Example: $E$ rises from 100 ¥/$ to 150 ¥/$ $\Rightarrow$ Dollar appreciated.
    - Dollar is now more expensive (foreigners must pay more yen to get one dollar).
- **Depreciation** ($E \downarrow$): Domestic currency loses value.
    - Example: $E$ falls from 150 ¥/$ to 100 ¥/$ $\Rightarrow$ Dollar depreciated.
    - Dollar is now cheaper (foreigners pay fewer yen per dollar).

#### Recent Example: Dollar-Yen (2022-2023)
- **Sharp Appreciation**: Dollar rose from ~100 ¥/$ to ~150 ¥/$ (massive 50% appreciation).
    - Driven by Fed tightening (raising US interest rates) while Bank of Japan kept rates near zero.
- **Intervention**: Bank of Japan intervened to slow yen depreciation (rare for major economies).

#### Limitations of Nominal Exchange Rate
- **Not Sufficient for Economic Decisions**: Knowing $E$ alone doesn't tell you where to buy goods.
    - Example: $E = 130$ ¥/$ tells you currency conversion rate.
    - But to decide whether to buy a car in US or Japan, you need to know **prices in both countries**.

### Real Exchange Rate ($\epsilon$)

#### Definition
$$\epsilon = \frac{E \times P}{P^*}$$

- $E$: Nominal exchange rate (foreign currency per unit of domestic currency).
- $P$: Domestic price level (price index or price of specific good).
- $P^*$: Foreign price level.

#### Interpretation
- **Relative Price of Goods**: How many units of foreign goods can one unit of domestic goods buy?
- **Purchasing Power**: The real exchange rate determines trade competitiveness.

#### Example: US vs. UK Car Purchase
- **US Car Price** ($P$): $10,000.
- **UK Car Price** ($P^*$): £8,000.
- **Exchange Rate** ($E$): 0.80 £/$ (1 dollar buys 0.80 pounds).

**Step 1**: Convert US price to pounds.
$$\text{US Car in Pounds} = P \times E = 10{,}000 \times 0.80 = £8{,}000$$

**Step 2**: Compare to UK price.
$$\epsilon = \frac{E \times P}{P^*} = \frac{0.80 \times 10{,}000}{8{,}000} = \frac{8{,}000}{8{,}000} = 1$$

**Interpretation**: One US car trades for exactly one UK car (equal purchasing power).

#### Alternative Scenario
- If $P^* = £9,000$ (UK car more expensive), then:
$$\epsilon = \frac{8,000}{9,000} = 0.89 < 1$$
- US car is **cheaper** than UK car $\Rightarrow$ Expect high US exports, low US imports.

- If $P^* = £7,000$ (UK car cheaper), then:
$$\epsilon = \frac{8,000}{7,000} = 1.14 > 1$$
- US car is **more expensive** $\Rightarrow$ Expect low US exports, high US imports.

#### Real Appreciation vs. Real Depreciation
- **Real Appreciation** ($\epsilon \uparrow$): Domestic goods become **relatively more expensive**.
    - **Causes**:
        - Nominal appreciation ($E \uparrow$).
        - Domestic inflation higher than foreign inflation ($P \uparrow$ faster than $P^* \uparrow$).
    - **Effects**:
        - Exports $\downarrow$ (foreign buyers find domestic goods expensive).
        - Imports $\uparrow$ (domestic buyers find foreign goods cheap).
- **Real Depreciation** ($\epsilon \downarrow$): Domestic goods become **relatively cheaper**.
    - **Causes**:
        - Nominal depreciation ($E \downarrow$).
        - Foreign inflation higher than domestic inflation.
    - **Effects**:
        - Exports $\uparrow$ (foreign buyers find domestic goods cheap).
        - Imports $\downarrow$ (domestic buyers find foreign goods expensive).

#### Importance of Real vs. Nominal
- **Trade Flows**: Determined by **real** exchange rate, not nominal.
    - Nominal exchange rate is only one component of $\epsilon$.
- **Nominal Volatility Dominates**: In practice, most short-run movements in $\epsilon$ come from changes in $E$ (prices $P, P^*$ adjust slowly).

## 5. Global Business Cycle Synchronization

```summary
- Recessions and expansions arrive together: 2008-2009 and 2020 were near-universal, and the 2001 dot-com downturn spread globally too
- **Trade linkages**: a US recession cuts US imports, which cuts foreign exports, which is a foreign recession
- **Financial linkages** and **confidence effects** do the same work, through global credit tightening and a hit to business and consumer confidence
- The asymmetry runs one way — US shocks reach everyone, while an Argentinian recession barely registers in the US
- China is now large enough to offer some diversification: its cycle is not perfectly correlated with the US, a stabilising force for commodity exporters
- Advanced economies track the global cycle closely; emerging markets grow faster on average but fluctuate in step
```

### Evidence of Synchronization
- **Business Cycles Move Together**: Recessions and expansions tend to occur simultaneously across countries.
    - 2008-2009 Global Financial Crisis: Nearly universal recession.
    - 2020 COVID Recession: Synchronized global contraction.
    - Smaller US recessions (e.g., 2001 dot-com) also spread globally.

### Mechanisms
1. **Trade Linkages**: US recession $\Rightarrow$ Lower US imports $\Rightarrow$ Lower foreign exports $\Rightarrow$ Foreign recession.
2. **Financial Linkages**: US financial crisis $\Rightarrow$ Global credit tightening $\Rightarrow$ Global demand contraction.
3. **Confidence Effects**: Crisis in large economy reduces global business/consumer confidence.

### Asymmetry
- **US to World**: Strong spillovers (US recession hurts everyone).
- **Small Country to US**: Weak spillovers (Argentinian recession has negligible effect on US).
- **Emerging Role of China**: China's size now provides some diversification.
    - China's cycle not perfectly correlated with US $\Rightarrow$ Stabilizing force for commodity exporters.

### Historical Data
- **Advanced Economies** (blue line): Track closely with global cycle.
- **Emerging Markets** (red line): Higher average growth (catch-up) but similar cyclical fluctuations.

## 6. Rising Global Integration

```summary
- US trade openness rose steadily from the 1970s through 2019: exports went from ~10% of GDP to ~15%, with imports rising alongside
- Cross-border financial holdings grew faster still
- Smaller countries are more open, because a limited domestic range of goods forces imports — Chile's trade ratio far exceeds that of the US or Japan
- Europe is the exception among large economies, and only because intra-European trade carries no currency or tariff barrier; treated as one entity, Europe is about as open as the US
- Post-2020 pressures — COVID, US-China tensions, supply chain reshoring — may have reversed the trend, though the integration built up over decades remains
```

### Historical Trend: Globalization
- **US Trade Openness**: Steady increase in $(X + IM)/GDP$ from 1970s through 2019.
    - Exports rose from ~10% of GDP to ~15%.
    - Imports rose similarly.
- **Financial Integration**: Even more dramatic increase in cross-border asset holdings.

### Size Matters
- **Smaller Countries $\Rightarrow$ More Open**:
    - Limited domestic variety of goods $\Rightarrow$ Must import.
    - Example: Chile (small economy) has much higher trade ratio than US or Japan.
- **Europe Anomaly**: Large European countries (Germany, France) have very high openness ratios.
    - **Reason**: Intra-European trade (same currency zone, minimal trade barriers).
    - If Europe treated as single entity, openness ratio similar to US.

### Recent Developments (Post-2020)
- **Deglobalization Pressures**: COVID, US-China tensions, supply chain reshoring.
- **Uncertain Future**: Trend toward openness may have reversed, but structural integration remains high.

## 7. Summary and Preview

```summary
- Three dimensions of openness — goods, financial markets and factor mobility — with this course focused on the first two
- **Nominal $E$** prices the currency and moves financial returns; **real $\epsilon$** prices the goods and moves trade; $NX = X - IM$ is where the two meet
- Cycles synchronize, so policy in a large economy has spillovers and, in an open economy, partly leaks abroad through imports and capital flows
- The exchange rate becomes a policy tool in its own right: depreciation boosts net exports
- Next: Lecture 19 opens the IS model to trade, Lectures 20-21 add financial openness through Mundell-Fleming, and Lecture 22 turns to expectations and asset pricing
```

### Key Concepts Introduced
1. **Three Dimensions of Openness**:
    - Goods markets (trade).
    - Financial markets (capital flows).
    - Factor markets (migration, FDI).
2. **Exchange Rates**:
    - **Nominal** ($E$): Price of currency (affects financial returns).
    - **Real** ($\epsilon$): Relative price of goods (affects trade flows).
3. **Trade Balance**: $NX = X - IM$ (influenced by $\epsilon, Y, Y^*$).
4. **Global Linkages**: Business cycles synchronized; policies in large economies have spillovers.

### Why This Matters
- **Policy Effectiveness**: Fiscal/monetary policy effects differ in open vs. closed economies.
    - Open economy: Policy "leaks" abroad through imports and capital flows.
    - Smaller multipliers, but new policy channels (exchange rate).
- **External Shocks**: Foreign demand, foreign interest rates, exchange rate movements are new sources of fluctuations.
- **Exchange Rate as Policy Tool**: Can use $\epsilon$ to stimulate demand (depreciation boosts net exports).

### What's Next
- **Lecture 19**: Extend IS model to open economy (goods market equilibrium with trade).
- **Lecture 20-21**: Introduce financial openness (Mundell-Fleming model: IS-LM with open capital account).
- **Lecture 22+**: Expectations and asset pricing, following on from exchange rate dynamics.

**Bottom Line**: Opening the economy introduces powerful new linkages and policy tradeoffs. For most countries, these are **first-order** considerations. Even for the US, they matter increasingly over time.
